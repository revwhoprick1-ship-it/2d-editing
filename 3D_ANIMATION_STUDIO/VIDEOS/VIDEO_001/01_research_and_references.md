# VIDEO_001 — Research & Scientific References

**Concept:** *What if you were shrunk and lived inside your own body?*

The story is fiction (nobody can be shrunk; at this scale a human could not breathe, think or survive). Every **biological fact** the narrator states is checked below against a credible source. Where a source could not be found, the claim was **removed from the script** rather than kept.

Sources were read on 2026‑11‑06 by a fetch agent working from the URLs below. Quotes are kept short and are used only to check facts. OpenStax text is CC BY‑NC‑SA, so none of its wording is reused in the narration.

## Derived (reasoned) statements

* **"A wall over a hundred times thinner than paper."** Membrane ≈ 0.5 µm (F7). Ordinary copy paper is about 0.1 mm = 100 µm thick (a common reference value, not taken from the sources above). 100 / 0.5 = 200, so "over a hundred times" is conservative.
* **A neutrophil is too wide for the narrowest capillaries.** Neutrophils are 10–12 µm (F9) and capillaries are 5–10 µm (F4), so the neutrophil has to deform to get through. The animation shows it squeezing. No speed or "sequestration" claim is made, because no source for one could be fetched.
* **Oxygenated blood looks brighter red.** Hemoglobin changes colour with oxygen. The shader shift is kept subtle and is not narrated.

## Fiction vs fact — how the video keeps them apart

| Fiction (story device) | Fact (narrated or shown) |
|---|---|
| Being shrunk, breathing, staying conscious | RBC size and shape, capillary width, valves, gas exchange direction |
| Riding a blood cell like a cushion | Blood cells moving with the flow and squeezing through capillaries |
| A neutrophil "noticing" you and keeping a shoe | Neutrophils engulf foreign material (phagocytosis) and digest it with enzymes |
| Cells drawn in convention colours (blue O₂, grey CO₂ particles, translucent white cells) | Real molecules are colourless and far smaller; the colours are a teaching convention |

## Psychology references behind the hook choice (no retention numbers are claimed)

* Loewenstein, G. (1994). *The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation.* Psychological Bulletin, 116(1), 75–98. This is the information‑gap theory: a specific, noticed gap in knowledge motivates people to close it.
* Kang, M. J., et al. (2009). *The wick in the candle of learning: Epistemic curiosity activates reward circuitry and enhances memory.* Psychological Science, 20(8), 963–973.
* Gruber, M. J., Gelman, B. D., & Ranganath, C. (2014). *States of curiosity modulate hippocampus‑dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit.* Neuron, 84(2), 486–496. Information learned while curious was remembered better.
* Green, M. C., & Brock, T. C. (2000). *The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives.* Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79(5), 701–721. Being "transported" into a story increases engagement.

How these shape the script: the opening line opens a concrete gap ("why would my own body attack me?") and promises it will close. Second‑person narration supports transportation into the story. The facts sit inside a survival story instead of a list.
